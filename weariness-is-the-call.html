When Weariness Is the Call, Not Its Failure

We treat exhaustion as a sign that something has gone wrong. But the most durable motivation in Scripture doesn't begin with excitement — it begins with a grief that will not lift.

There is a moment, familiar to almost everyone who has served for any length of time, when the energy runs out. The work that once felt like a calling begins to feel like a weight. And the quiet, frightening question arrives: have I lost it? Was I ever really called to this at all?

The instinct, when this happens, is to reach for more enthusiasm — to try to manufacture the feeling that made the work meaningful in the beginning. We look for the conference, the sabbatical, the fresh vision that will reignite what has gone cold. And sometimes those things help. But often they don't, because they are aimed at the wrong thing. They assume the problem is a shortage of excitement. The book of Nehemiah suggests the problem may be that we mistook excitement for motivation in the first place.

Nehemiah begins with weeping, not vision

When Nehemiah hears that the walls of Jerusalem lie in ruins, he does not respond with a strategic plan or a surge of energy. He sits down and weeps. He mourns for days. He fasts and prays. Notice the timeline: this grief comes months before he lifts a single stone — before he has a plan, before he has permission, before there is any prospect of the work actually beginning.

The call was always hidden inside the burden you couldn't put down.

And notice something else, easy to miss: Nehemiah had never lived in Jerusalem. He was a cupbearer in the Persian court, born in exile, comfortable, established. The ruined city was not his problem to carry. But he could not put it down. That is the shape of real motivation — not a feeling that energises you toward something attractive, but a specific brokenness you find you cannot walk past, even when walking past it would be entirely reasonable.

Daniel Goleman, whose framework of emotional intelligence runs through Emotionally Whole, defines motivation as the drive that keeps a person moving toward a goal when the external rewards have stopped arriving. That is a useful description. But Scripture presses further into where that drive comes from — and in Nehemiah's case, it comes from grief, not gladness.

Why this reframes burnout

If motivation is fundamentally about enthusiasm, then the loss of enthusiasm is the loss of motivation, and burnout is a kind of failure — a sign you are no longer suited to the work. This is what many faithful leaders quietly believe about themselves, and it is why so many leave.

But if durable motivation is rooted in a burden rather than a feeling, then the disappearance of excitement is not the disappearance of the call. The excitement was always going to fade; it was scaffolding, not foundation. What remains when it is gone — the grief that will not lift, the specific people or problem you still cannot stop caring about even when you are tired of caring — that is the actual call, finally visible now that the scaffolding has come down.

This does not make the weariness pleasant. Nehemiah's grief was real grief; it cost him something. But it reframes what the weariness means. It is not evidence that you have failed. It may be evidence that you are, at last, motivated by the right thing — the thing underneath the excitement, which was always the point.

The honest question to sit with

So the question to ask in the tired season is not how do I get my excitement back? It is quieter and more searching: what is the grief I cannot put down? Not the task list, not the obligations that exhaust you, but the specific brokenness that, when you are honest, still moves you. That is where the call has been living all along.

Nehemiah rebuilt a wall in fifty-two days. But the wall was never the source of his motivation. The wall was the evidence of it. The source was a grief he carried, in silence, for months before anyone knew — a burden that turned out to be a calling wearing work clothes.
