Foreword

A word before you begin

Emotionally Whole by Winston Chew guides readers toward understanding and becoming emotionally healthy individuals. In doing so, it also equips them to minister to others who may be emotionally unaware, fragile, or broken.

Winston draws from his own experiences with his parents and his personal journey with emotional intelligence through the years; his reflections carry the authenticity of a message that arises directly from life. He unfolds his chapters like a guided tour through the grand rooms of a palace, keeping readers engaged as the picture of emotional wholeness comes into view, and he conveys profound psychological and theological insights with clarity and simplicity.

The book's framework is grounded in Daniel Goleman's Emotional Intelligence. What makes Winston's approach distinctive is how thoughtfully he weaves Scripture into that framework. Through biblical characters who exemplify emotional awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social intelligence, he directs us to authentic living and points us to the Redeemer God who heals broken humanity. The Soul Practice of each chapter cultivates this wholeness.

We are created as emotional beings. Yet many, especially men, remain emotionally unaware, suppressed, and deprived. True emotional well-being stems from nurturing three key relationships: with God, with ourselves, and with others. To be truly human, we must express our emotions in a healthy, redemptive way and come fully alive.

This is a valuable book for every reader — an invitation to wake up and grow into the emotionally whole person our Creator intends us to be.

— Rev. Dr. Thomas Chin,
Director of the Pastoral Renewal and Research Centre, Malaysian Baptist Theological Seminary

Prologue

Two Rooms

Father and Mother

I grew up in a house that held two portraits of emotional life, and the contrast between them has shaped everything I have since become.

My father ran a car-repair workshop. He was a hard worker and, I believe, a deeply frustrated man, though the source of his frustration only became clear to me much later. What I understood as a child was the sound of his raised voice, the unpredictability of his anger, and the way the atmosphere of our home shifted when he entered a room in a particular mood. I began helping him in the workshop at thirteen, often working past midnight so the vehicles would be ready for collection the next morning. I learned a great deal about engines in those years. The man whose hands worked alongside mine remained, across those same years, largely unread.

I know more now than I knew then, and what I know has changed what I see. When I visited him in the hospital near the end of his life — he died at forty-nine, when I was seventeen and my youngest sister was three — I braced myself to ask him if he would believe in Jesus. He told me that he was suffering like Jesus. That sentence was all he gave me. I've carried it for decades. In the years since, the framework I've spent my career developing has given me a fuller way to read the man I grew up with: a portrait of what an unformed emotional life costs a family, a man carrying something he had no language for. He had six children. He trained apprentices with diligence and honesty and watched them leave with what he had given them. He worked hard. He charged his customers honestly. He gave away skills that cost him to acquire. The thirteen-year-old working beside him had no framework for reading what he was watching. The man who grew from that boy has spent his career building one: not to explain his father, but to honour what his father carried in the dark.

My mother was different. She was a housewife (quiet, patient, kind) who prepared meals for my father's workers every day and absorbed his frustrations with composure, returning neither complaint nor retaliation. In all my memory of her, her voice remained gentle. As a young child, I used to lean against her back and cuddle her while she sat on a low stool making New Year cookies. The warmth of that memory remains with me until today.

When I was nineteen, I told her I wanted to leave home, to serve with Campus Crusade for Christ in Kuala Lumpur, answering a call rather than seeking a career. It was my first answer to a call I was only beginning to understand. I asked her permission. She said, "Son, you are big enough to make your own decision. It's okay, you can go." Her blessing was her love made audible, and it was the first time someone sent me into ministry.

Some years into my ministry, my mother received Jesus as her Lord and Saviour. I had the privilege of baptising her myself, the son now immersing the mother who had once sent him. She who had sent me into ministry received, from that same Lord, grace of her own.

In her final years, she received dialysis, lost her eyesight, and remained, across every conversation I can recall, gracious, uncomplaining, and entirely free of self-pity. When I visited her in the hospital before she died, she took my hand and kissed it.

I've returned often in my mind to two moments with my mother. The first: the nineteen-year-old asking her blessing to leave, unaware of what the years ahead would cost, knowing only that he had heard something that would not leave him. The second: a man in his mid-thirties standing at a hospital bedside, holding the hand of a woman who could no longer see him, and feeling her lips press gently against it. She said nothing. The silence carried everything. Her blessing at nineteen sent me into ministry. Her silent kiss at the end completed what words, by then, could only approach. After both those moments had passed, it would take another twenty-five years before I finally found the language for what I had been living through.

I know now that she was living a very demanding form of empathy available to any human being: crossing toward others faithfully, at sustained personal cost, releasing the need for reciprocity or acknowledgement. Her formation preceded Goleman's framework by decades. She lived what it would later name. That was her theology, and she never needed the vocabulary to articulate it.

East Malaysia and Two Ministers

I was twenty-six years old, newly married, and certain I was ready when I left for East Malaysia.

I had graduated from seminary with twenty boxes of books shipped ahead of me to the congregation where I had been called to serve. The boxes were evidence of permanence: twenty boxes travel only with someone who intends to stay.

For several months, everything seemed to be going well. Fieldwork every week, door-to-door outreach, training members. I was young, energetic, and entirely convinced that sincerity and theological formation were sufficient preparation for whatever the ministry would ask of me.

Then I heard that the church leadership had been questioning my absence from the office.

The report landed in me like a verdict. The reaction came before any examination of it: a resignation filed before I had requested a meeting, before I had sought clarification, before I had stayed long enough to understand what was being said. I packed the boxes. My wife and I came home to West Malaysia.

What followed were disorienting years of my life: selling encyclopaedias, completing a Diploma in Hair Design, drifting through years whose name had not yet arrived. Beneath the drifting, I was depressed and deeply unsure whether the call I had answered so confidently had been real at all.

Grace came twice, and the sequence mattered.

In 1990, a senior pastor chose to see something in me that I could no longer see in myself and opened the door toward restoration. That act of seeing was its own form of grace. In the following year, a second senior minister invited me to serve alongside him in his ministry, a commission into something I had not yet built, rather than merely a return to what had been. I returned to ministry through two consecutive acts of grace from two different people. My record, at the time, recommended me to neither. I carry even now what it costs a person in authority to extend that kind of grace, and the particular mercy of having it extended not once but twice.

The language for what those two men were doing in EQ terms arrived only later, and this book is where it arrived. They were exercising the social intelligence this book's final chapter describes: influence without positional compulsion, the willingness to cross toward someone whose situation offered no strategic return, the ability to see what a person needed to become and to act on that seeing in ways that expanded rather than diminished what remained possible. They opened a door. The opening cost them something. What I built on the other side of it was built on ground that had to be given before it could be built on: ground prepared by grace rather than by anything I had done to deserve it.

Twenty years later, that church in East Malaysia invited me back. Some of the members had never known why I left, some had wondered quietly whether they were the reason. We reconciled. All was forgiven. What I could not yet fully explain, grace covered anyway.

The language came later. Recently, some of those same people asked to hear the inner story, the real account of what had happened in that young pastor's interior on the day he packed his boxes and came home. And I was finally able to tell them: the fault was entirely on my end. A young man whose emotional life had not yet been formed enough to carry the weight of what he had been given. A reaction rather than a response. A self-image that collapsed at the first serious point of pressure because it had never been honestly examined.

They received it with more grace than I deserved: which is, I've come to understand, precisely how grace works.

That sequence — reconciliation before explanation, forgiveness before full understanding, the naming that arrives only after the language has been formed — is, in miniature, what this book is about. But to explain how the language finally came, I need to tell you about another furnace.

Cardiac Episode

In January 2001, at four o'clock in the morning, I woke to a cardiac episode whose outcome, in those first minutes, I held as uncertain.

At that time, my four children were aged thirteen, eight, three, and one. An angiogram showed no blockages; my heart was structurally healthy. The cardiologists concluded that the episode had been caused by a severely stressful working environment and a range of demotivating factors that had accumulated over years. After my discharge, I went through six months of anxiety: fearing another similar episode whenever I tried to sleep, unable to feel pleasure, fighting daily against a fear of dying that I could not reason my way out of.

I was surviving. Living was what I was working back towards.

I thought about my children constantly. I thought about what my father's workshop had taught me about engines — that they can appear to be functioning while something fundamental is failing beneath the surface. And I thought, for the first time with real urgency, about what it means for a human being to be running on reserves that have already run out.

Following my cardiologist's counsel, I moved into a different kind of life, one with enough room to breathe, enough quiet to listen. I began training in areas where I had long relied on instinct alone, pursuing formal qualifications in counselling and human development.

What grew from those years was a response to a question my own life had spent decades posing: and that I began to hear in every counselling room I entered.

Why does a gifted person keep arriving at the same breaking point? Why does clarity of calling not protect us from interior collapse? Why does the emotional life so often run ahead of, or fall behind, the life we are trying to build? And beneath all of these: what is happening in the interior of a person, and what does it take to form that interior into something durable enough to carry the life it has been given?

Formation is the answer. And formation keeps its own pace, holding its shape against every impulse to hurry it.

The Repair Instinct and Closing Offer

From the time I was a small boy, I've been drawn to repairing things. I once put a clock into an aquarium just to understand how it would sound when the alarm rang underwater. I helped my father fix engines in the dark after midnight. I've spent decades doing DIY repairs around the house (plumbing, electrical fittings, broken chairs) because something in me is drawn towards what is broken and wants to understand how it works and whether it can be made whole.

That same instinct, I've come to understand, is what brought me into the counselling room and the coaching session and the pastoral conversation, and, across fifteen years of corporate consulting, into boardrooms where the formation need was identical and the language for it had not yet arrived. I'm drawn towards what is broken in human beings: drawn to understand its mechanics and to offer, where I can, the slow and careful work of repair: engaging rather than observing, entering rather than managing from a distance.

This book is that instinct applied to its deepest question: what breaks the interior life of a leader, a pastor, a parent, a human being: and what does formation towards wholeness look like?

The biblical characters in these pages are people I recognise: in my counselling room, in my mirror, and in the house where I grew up. And the framework I've used to examine their lives — Goleman's emotional intelligence domains — is the lens I've used to see more clearly what Scripture has always shown about the lives we are called to lead.

I offer this book as a practitioner. I offer it as someone who has lived several of its chapters in my own biography before the language for what I was living through had arrived. And I offer it as someone who has been personally warned, by a cardiologist in 2001, that the life I was living demanded change I had not yet chosen. The years since have been, among other things, a slow and sometimes painful education in what sustainable formation requires.

My mother knew. She lived it on instinct, without instruction. Her hand in mine, raised to her lips in a hospital bed near the end of her life, was the last lesson she gave me. It came unbidden and unearned — the way grace always comes: freely, and from above.

I've carried two hospital rooms with me for as long as I can remember. In one, a man who worked past midnight, charged his customers honestly, and gave away skills that had cost him to acquire, a man who handed his thirteen-year-old son an unasked question and left before the answer came. In the other, a woman who could no longer see the son whose hand she was holding, who pressed her lips against it and gave him everything words, by then, could only approach. One room taught me what the interior life costs when it is left unformed. The other showed me what it looks like when formation has done its quiet, decades-long work. This book grew from both.

It is, among other things, my attempt to pass on what those rooms taught me. The characters in the pages ahead have their own rooms; their own furnaces, their own silences, their own moments when the interior life was brought to the edge of what it could carry. I invite you to read them as I've learned to read my father and my mother: not as examples to follow, but as witnesses to what God does in a human being who has run out of everything except honesty (see Ps 51:17; Isa 64:8; 2 Cor 12:9–10).

Introduction

The woman at the doorway

A woman stands in the temple at Shiloh, as she has stood there year after year, carrying the same ache. This time she has reached the end of what she can carry alone.

First Samuel tells us she is "in bitterness of soul" (1 Sam 1:10). Her sorrow has moved beyond a private disappointment. It has become part of the way she wakes, prays, and sits among others. Her husband loves her; his love cannot take the ache away. Her rival wounds her again. She comes before God, unsure perhaps how much longer she can keep bringing the same grief. She prays in silence, her lips moving while tears come. Eli the priest watches from his seat by the doorpost and misunderstands her completely.

He speaks from his office and from what he thinks he sees: "How long will you be drunk? Put your wine away from you!" (1 Sam 1:14).

Hannah answers with quiet dignity:

"No, my lord, I am a woman of sorrowful spirit. I have drunk neither wine nor intoxicating drink, but have poured out my soul before the LORD" (1 Sam 1:15).

Hannah isn't describing a carefully managed prayer. She has poured out her soul before the LORD. Something in her has been emptied before God instead of held back or kept under control. She has brought the whole weight of herself into God's presence. After this, her face was no longer sad (1 Sam 1:18). Her situation had not yet changed. Something in her had.

Hannah doesn't collapse under Eli's accusation. She doesn't pretend he is right because he is the priest. She names herself truthfully: "I am a woman of sorrowful spirit." Then she tells him where her sorrow has gone: "I have poured out my soul before the LORD." Her self-knowledge is held in the presence of God. She knows what is happening inside her because she has brought it honestly before God who alone can hold it. Eli has misread her. Hannah has not misread herself. She enters the temple in bitterness; she doesn't leave it the same, and it's written on her face.

Scripture doesn't explain the mystery at length. It shows us a woman who brought her sorrow to God and was met there.

Many of us know something about that. There are times when the inner life feels too large to carry alone. There are prayers we bring to God before we can explain them even to ourselves. There are moments when someone who should have understood us reads us wrongly, and we have to find the courage to speak truthfully. Hannah stands at the doorway of this book because she shows us where emotional honesty begins: before God, without performance.

The weightiest moments in this book come when its characters fail. David: a king intimate enough with God to write Psalm 23, blind enough to arrange a man's death and call it governance (2 Sam 11:14–17). Peter at the courtyard fire: three denials in the cold and the sound of a rooster doing what his own voice had failed to do (Luke 22:54–62). Paul and Barnabas, a sharpness between them sufficient to end what they had built together (Acts 15:39). Nehemiah's fist on the table, the rage of a man who had rebuilt walls only to find the rot had come from inside (Neh 5:6). These aren't the cautionary sections. These are the places where the deepest work of the book becomes possible — because they show what God does in a human being who has run out of everything except honesty.

Joseph's story runs differently. Joseph in the pit, Joseph in the prison — more years of both than the dream had prepared him for (Gen 37:24; Gen 39:20). Not a failure of character, but a furnace that tested it. The formation is the same. The ground beneath it is the same. What God does in a person who has run out of everything except the willingness to remain looks identical from the outside, whether the fire was lit by their own failure or by someone else's injustice.

Chapter 4's characters offer no failure moment that honest exegesis can sustain, so the failure that chapter examines is the reader's own: depletion. Chapter 5's characters offer none either; there the rupture belongs to the reader who has been present, capable, and consistently unheard. The ground beneath both is the same as every other chapter. Grace has nothing to work with in a character who never fails, so where honest exegesis finds no failure, these chapters turn toward the reader's own. Restoration deepens only as far as the rupture is truthfully acknowledged.

Hannah doesn't appear as one of the chapter subjects in this book. She opens the way because her moment in the temple shows, with great tenderness, what emotional honesty can look like when a person brings her whole self before God.

Two convictions guide the pages that follow. Scripture is the ground on which this work stands; the biblical narratives are where we listen first. Emotional intelligence language has helped me notice patterns of human relating with clarity, but it remains a servant to the reading. Scripture carries the authority.

Why Biblical Characters?

The Bible gives us the story of a God who creates, calls, redeems, and restores human beings made in his image. The emotional lives of the men and women in Scripture aren't the main point by themselves. God's purposes remain central. Yet those purposes are worked out through real people: people who fear, grieve, resist, fail, hope, and continue to be formed. This book listens to those lives carefully.

Scripture remains first. Emotional intelligence terms help describe what Scripture already shows, but they never govern the Scripture. When they clarify, I use them. When they limit, I name the limit. When they conflict with Scripture's vision of the human person, Scripture leads.

A word, here at the outset, about the book's hermeneutical posture toward the women in these pages. Ruth, Lydia, and Priscilla are read as full participants in the formation narrative of Scripture, carrying the same exegetical weight as the men alongside whom they appear. This book reads their gifting and formation as unrestricted by gender: a conviction held with full awareness that faithful, Scripture-honouring interpreters continue to reach different conclusions on the questions that surround it. Where those questions press hardest, in Acts 18:26 and the texts around it, the Endnotes set out the interpretive ground in detail. The request made of every reader is modest: let these women be heard before the framework is debated. The exegesis belongs to them. The hermeneutical conversation belongs to the whole church.

Each character study is organised around three relationships named in the Great Commandment: with God, with ourselves, and with others (Matt 22:37–39). These relationships are lived together, not completed in sequence. Emotional formation touches all three.

Hannah came to the temple in bitterness of soul. She poured out what she was carrying and left with her face no longer sad. Her circumstances had not yet changed. Something in her had been entrusted to God. The people in the chapters ahead have their own temples, their own moments of honesty, and their own places where God meets them. We do too. Read their stories slowly and let them give you courage for your own.

Chapter One · Self-Awareness

The Mirror

The Gap

People thought of you as someone who knew yourself well.

That was the quiet agreement in many of the rooms you entered — at work, at church, among the people who knew you closely. You'd done the work: the assessments, the difficult conversations, the leadership coaching, the honest naming of your weaknesses. People trusted your ability to say, "I was wrong about that," and mean it. If someone had been asked to name one of your strengths, self-awareness would likely have been near the top.

What you couldn't see was the version of yourself that showed up when you weren't in the room. The version your team spoke about quietly after difficult meetings. The version the people closest to you had grown tired of trying to explain, because the conversation so often ended in the same place. That version of you wasn't entirely false, and neither was the version you believed about yourself. But there was a gap between them, and the gap mattered.

You thought you knew yourself. In many ways, you did.

Then someone helped you see what you'd missed.

For me, that mirror often came through accusation — the kind that arrives suddenly and unsettles something deep. A stray comment, a misread intention, a story repeated without checking, and something in me would flare. My reaction was sharp and immediate, often larger than the moment required. I told myself it was about truth and integrity. Over time, I began to see the accusation wasn't the deepest issue. My reaction was showing me something. It touched the part of me that couldn't bear being misunderstood, the part that needed to be seen as trustworthy and above suspicion. Mirrors often work like that. They reveal the defended self, the reactive self, the self we've learned to explain or ignore. Healing often begins in those small, unguarded moments when we see the distance between who we believe ourselves to be and who we become when something presses an unhealed place.

David knew that place. So did Peter. If you've found your way to this page, you may know something of it too.

Sometimes the moment itself is ordinary. A conversation after a meeting. A careful word from someone you trust. A sentence that lands harder than the speaker may have intended. Then the choice comes quietly: look, or look away.

One sentence, easy to read past: "But David remained at Jerusalem" (2 Sam 11:1). Every other king in the region is at war. David sent his army, his commanders, his best general. And stayed home. Nothing dramatic happens yet. He's simply not where a king is required to be.

That's the gap, and David is standing in it before he's done anything wrong. In this single unremarkable sentence, he has already begun the drift that ends on a roof and in a dead man's tent. Nothing about the sentence announces what's coming. That's what makes it worth pausing on.

He had no mirror held up yet, no accusation to flinch from, nothing but the ordinary decision to stay instead of go. Whatever that decision was already forming in him, it had years yet to run before anyone — David included — would recognise it for what it was.

The Ancient Problem with a Modern Name

"Know thyself," the ancient instruction traditionally associated with the Oracle at Delphi, continues to be one of the hardest things a person can attempt. We have been trying to do it for a very long time. Many of us discover, sooner or later, that knowing ourselves isn't as simple as looking inward.

When Daniel Goleman wrote Emotional Intelligence in 1995, he gave fresh language to this old struggle. He described self-awareness as the first and foundational domain of emotional intelligence: the ability to recognise our own emotions, see our strengths and limitations with some accuracy, and understand how our inner life affects the people around us.

The definition sounds clear enough on the page; living up to it is a different matter entirely.

Part of the difficulty is that the very mind that helps us move through life also protects the story we've learned to tell about ourselves. We make quick judgments. We act under pressure. We keep together a sense of who we are. These are necessary gifts. Yet the same inner habits can filter what we notice. We often receive information about ourselves through a self-image already in place: one that has taken years to build and does not welcome questions about its accuracy.

Goleman, Boyatzis, and McKee used the word dissonance for the painful gap that can form when a leader's inner life and outward impact begin to separate. Over time, people around such a leader may experience tension, weariness, or even toxicity, while the leader remains largely unaware of what is being produced. Their research confirms what Scripture has long shown: we often need an honest voice from outside ourselves. The same confidence that helps a person lead can also make it hard to receive information that challenges the self-image they've depended on.

That's why self-awareness matters so much. The gap grows quietly: between what we meant and what others experienced, between the person we believe ourselves to be and the person others meet, between what we are ready to see and what we've learned to leave untouched. Closing that gap asks for a posture of humility. It means becoming willing to receive from outside ourselves what our own inner life may keep postponing.

The Paradox of the Mirror

Self-awareness carries a strange difficulty within it. The people who need a mirror are often the people very certain they already see themselves clearly.

Competence can quietly strengthen the story we tell about ourselves. We have evidence: sound judgments, hard stretches survived, results that seem to confirm who we believe we are. So, when a question finally comes — perhaps this self-image is incomplete — it doesn't arrive into an empty room. It comes against years of reasons for trusting ourselves. The question may be true. The resistance to it may also feel reasonable.

Goleman's framework helps us tell two things apart that can look similar. One is emotional fluency, the ability to name and talk about what we feel. The other is the harder work of noticing our patterns accurately and understanding how those patterns affect other people. A person can speak with great emotional language and yet remain unaware of what happens when they walk into a room under pressure: of what their presence stirs up in others, what they repeat, or what version of themselves appears when the stakes are high.

Self-awareness becomes difficult in the gap between how we present ourselves and how we function. The same confidence that helps us lead, decide, and act can also make us slow to receive anything that unsettles the image we've depended on for years.

Sometimes what looks like stubbornness is also the mind protecting itself under pressure. Goleman, drawing on Joseph LeDoux's research on emotional processing, observed that our threat responses can move faster than our conscious awareness: we may react before we've fully understood what's happening, and the reaction can feel entirely justified.

We all know moments like that. We feel clear, strong, honest. Yet Scripture reminds us that certainty can conceal the places where our sight narrows. David poured his inner life into the Psalms, and remained blind to what was forming in him between those prayers. The convictions we hold tightly may need a softer, slower look. The identities we guard fiercely may be carrying wounds that remain unidentified. Grace reveals these places because freedom cannot take root where truth is withheld.

When the Foundation Is Missing

Goleman placed self-awareness at the foundation of emotional intelligence for good reason. When we can't see our own emotional patterns, the rest of our life is built on uncertain ground. We may react and call it decision-making. We may carry an unexamined wound into someone else's story and think we're being empathetic. We may bear a self-image that people close to us know is only partly true and then build relationships on it. So much depends on whether we're willing to see ourselves with some accuracy.

Self-awareness asks for accuracy. It asks us to keep the gap between self-image and reality open long enough for truth to teach us what we haven't yet been ready to know. Sometimes the mirror is held by someone else. Sometimes it arrives before we've invited it. Sometimes it touches the very part of ourselves we've spent years protecting. Even then, the invitation is to keep looking without rushing to defend ourselves.

That kind of willingness is spiritual as well as emotional.

We were made in the image of God, imago Dei (Gen 1:26–27): part of that dignity is the ability to stand in the light of God and know ourselves truthfully. Sin hasn't erased that ability, but it has bent it: pride and shame both distort the mirror. Self-awareness, in the biblical sense, is part of God's restoring work in us, bringing us back toward the kind of seeing we were made for.

When the mirror is finally held up, it will show us where we are. If we keep looking, it may also help us see what God is calling us back toward. The gap between those two realities is often where grace begins its patient work. Jesus' story of the prodigal son gives us a tender picture of this. The son "came to himself" when he saw the distance between his hunger and his father's house (Luke 15:17).

Luke's phrase "came to himself" carries more weight than it may first seem. The Greek expression, ēlthen eis heauton, suggests a return to one's own person, a recovering of one's senses, a coming back to the self that had been living at a distance from truth. The famine made the prodigal's old self-image impossible to maintain. He saw where he was. He remembered where he belonged. That seeing was already the beginning of the way home.

We see the same pattern elsewhere in Scripture. Isaiah saw the Lord "high and lifted up," and the first words that came from him were words of honest self-seeing: "Woe is me, for I am undone! Because I am a man of unclean lips" (Isa 6:5). Paul, many years into fruitful ministry, could confess, "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am chief" (1 Tim 1:15). These aren't moments of self-hatred. They're moments when the presence of God gives a person the courage to see truthfully. The closer the light comes, the more clearly the self is seen.

God often meets his people in the very places where they feel divided: between fear and obedience, sorrow and hope, the person they are and the person they are being called to become. When we stand there ready to submit, we usually discover that grace has already been nearer than we knew. Self-awareness is never something we practise alone. It lives in three relationships at once.

First, there is our relationship with God. We grow in self-awareness as we become willing to be fully known by our Creator who made us. That includes the version of ourselves that appears in the spaces between prayers. If we examine ourselves only by looking inward, we will usually find ways to manage what we're not ready to see. God's presence gives self-awareness its truest light.

Second, there is our relationship with ourselves, where Goleman's work is especially helpful: noticing our emotional patterns, recognising the gap between our intention and our impact, and learning to stay present to what the mirror shows without immediately flinching or fleeing. This work is real and demanding. It also needs to be held before God.

Third, there is our relationship with others. Nathan came to David. Jesus turned and looked at Peter. In both stories, the mirror came through another person: through a word, or through a gaze, carried with painful love. Many of us won't come to see ourselves clearly without the presence of someone who loves us enough to speak truthfully.

David and Peter help us see all three relationships at work. Their stories aren't neat examples for us to copy. They're witnesses to what happens when God brings a person, painfully and mercifully, into honest self-knowledge.

David: The Man Who Needed a Mirror

The Man Who Knew God and Did Not Know Himself

Before we arrive at the throne room and Nathan's parable, something demands our attention, and it's more uncomfortable than the moral failure itself: no one in the biblical record articulates his inner life to God with greater depth than David.

The Psalms are the evidence: poems spanning lament and praise, terror and trust, fury at enemies and tenderness toward God. David gave God everything. He wept. He raged. He exulted. He let his full interior world be seen, and he was seen.

By Goleman's definition, this is a man of deep emotional fluency. The paradox it turns on is precise: emotional fluency and emotional self-awareness are distinct competencies, and a person can be gifted in one while remaining nearly blind in the other.

David could articulate his feelings to God with unflinching honesty and depth and remain, with the same unbending consistency, blind to what he was doing to the people around him. The Psalms show us a man transparent before God about every interior state; the narrative of 2 Sam 11 shows us the same man arranging Uriah's death with the narrative recording no pause. The gap between those two portraits is the paradox. His intimacy with God was vertical and real; what it hadn't yet produced was formation in the fullest biblical sense. The narrative that follows shows what that gap looks like from the inside.

Here is something more disturbing than hypocrisy. Hypocrisy at least requires self-knowledge; it knows what it's concealing. What 2 Sam 11 gives us is a man who acts, covers, arranges, and moves forward — and the narrative records no pause. The dangerous blindness is the kind that shows itself in a person who looks, in every outward way, spiritually formed.

The Long Blindness — 2 Samuel 11

"It happened in the spring of the year, at the time when kings go out to battle, that David sent Joab and his servants with him, and all Israel... But David remained at Jerusalem" (2 Sam 11:1).

David is absent from the place a king is required to be. He has sent others to bear the cost he should be carrying. Whatever military reasoning lay behind the decision, the EQ portrait is already taking shape: a man operating from a position of unaccountable power, increasingly insulated from the consequences of his own authority.

What follows is narrated with clinical economy. He sees a woman bathing. He sends for her. He sleeps with her. She conceives. He attempts to conceal the pregnancy by arranging her husband Uriah's return from the front. When that fails — because Uriah is a man of honour who refuses to eat and drink while his fellow soldiers remain in the field — David arranges Uriah's death. He places him at the front of the battle and withdraws the other men. Uriah is killed. The sequence closes with a quiet, devastating sentence: "But the thing that David had done displeased the Lord" (2 Sam 11:27).

This is the opposite of the interior life the Psalms record. There's no indication of crisis here: no anguish, no sleeplessness, no private reckoning with what has been done. Instead, he normalizes it: "Do not let this thing displease you" (2 Sam 11:25): a line addressed to Joab but functioning as its own quiet self-justification: this is what war costs. Divine perception stands over human blindness. The gap between those two — God seeing everything David had done, and David registering none of it — is the governing portrait of self-awareness failure.

From an EQ perspective, the arresting reality is the scale of the impact David leaves behind, and his apparent blindness to it. Bathsheba is named, situated, and then reduced to a piece in a cover-up. Uriah is named, described with precision, and shown — in painful contrast — to be the man of honour David has ceased to be. He refuses the comfort David is trying to manufacture through him. In the middle of 2 Sam 11, he is the self-aware presence — and that very clarity is what ends his life. The relational cost of David's blindness has a name. Two names.

The Rupture — 2 Samuel 12

Nathan comes to David with a story.

A rich man and a poor man lived in the same city. The rich man had vast flocks and herds. The poor man had nothing except one small ewe lamb he had bought and raised from birth: it ate from his table, drank from his cup, slept in his arms. It was like a daughter to him. When a traveller came to visit the rich man, he reached past his own abundant flocks and took the poor man's lamb instead.

Nathan stops there, offering nothing further — no application, no direct accusation, just the story left standing on its own.

David's response is immediate, furious, and entirely uncomplicated: "the man who has done this shall surely die! And he shall restore fourfold for the lamb, because he did this thing and because he had no pity" (2 Sam 12:5–6).

The verdict comes without hesitation, delivered by a judge who has no idea yet whose case he's ruling on.

Nathan then delivered the verdict David had walked into without knowing it: "You are the man!" (2 Sam 12:7). Four words, and the distance between judge and judged was gone.

This moment deserves its full weight. Nathan has just performed an act of unusual psychological and pastoral sophistication. Direct confrontation of a king in the grip of self-deception reliably produces defensiveness, rationalisation, counter-accusation — the very mechanisms that have kept the self-image intact. Nathan told a story instead. A story carefully engineered to bypass David's defences by enlisting David's own moral clarity as the instrument of his undoing.

David walked into the parable as its judge. He helped set the mirror in place.

The gap between "he shall surely die" and "you are the man" is the gap in which true self-awareness finally arrives: sudden, total, irreversible. The mirror is held up. The gap closes. In a single shattering instant of recognition, the distance between self-image and reality collapses, and the collapse is complete before David has had time to prepare a defence against it.

David's response is immediate and undefended: "I have sinned against the Lord" (2 Sam 12:13).
